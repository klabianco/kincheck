KinCheck Protocol

Two people who have met in person each hold the same printed table of one-time word lines. To check who they're talking to over any voice or text channel, one reads a line number and its ask word, the other replies with that line's two answer words, and both cross the line out.

The protocol needs no device, account, network or operator. A cloned voice or face does not help an attacker, because the check is about what each person holds, not how they sound. This document defines the table, the exchange and the rules that make it hold up against an attacker who can call everyone involved.

The keywords MUST, MUST NOT, SHOULD and MAY are used as in RFC 2119.

1Overview

A pair is two people, A and B. A pair has exactly one table, printed twice: A's copy and B's copy. A person in several pairs holds one table per pair. Grandma, Alex and Sam need three tables, which is six printed pages.

Each table has numbered lines. A line has one ask word and two answer words. Every line is used at most once, whether the check succeeds or fails.

Generated in your browser from crypto.getRandomValues. Nothing is sent or stored.

2The table

  1. A table MUST belong to exactly one pair, and MUST exist in exactly two copies. No line may appear on any other table. This is the rule that defeats the cross-relative attack in §4.
  2. Each word MUST be drawn uniformly at random from the v1 word list (§6) with a cryptographically secure generator. Words MUST NOT repeat within a table (draw without replacement).
  3. A table SHOULD have 30 lines and MUST have at least 10. When fewer than 5 lines remain, the pair SHOULD make a new table.
  4. Each copy MUST print both names (holder and partner), so the right table is picked up quickly under pressure. It SHOULD print a set identifier and the date it was made, and the rules in §3 in plain language.
  5. A table SHOULD exist only on paper. A generator MUST NOT transmit or store the table. Copies SHOULD be handed over in person.

3The check

The person being asked for something (money, a code, access, an urgent favour) is the asker. The other person is the answerer.

Asker"Card check: line 4, pancake."
Answererfinds line 4, sees the ask word matches
Answerer"Glacier, harbor."
Bothcross out line 4

Asker

  1. MUST use their lowest-numbered line that isn't crossed out, and MUST say the ask word with the line number.
  2. MUST treat a wrong answer, a stall or "I lost my card" as a failed check: end the call and reach the person on a contact already known to the asker.
  3. MUST cross the line out after any attempt, including a failed one.
  4. If the answerer says the line is already crossed out on their copy, the asker MUST cross it out too and use their next line.

Answerer

  1. MUST NOT read any word from the table unless the asker first gave the correct ask word for a line that isn't crossed out on the answerer's copy.
  2. MUST cross that line out after answering.
  3. A correct ask word does not prove who the asker is (§4, cross-pair leak). If the asker then asks the answerer for something, the answerer MUST run their own check on their next line.

The check MAY run over text messages in the same form. It SHOULD NOT run over a channel that both parties' copies could be photographed into, such as a shared cloud photo album.

4Threat model

The attacker can reproduce any participant's voice and face, call or message anyone at any time, spoof caller ID, and record every check. The attacker cannot see the paper copies. The analysis uses three people (A, B, C) because the important attack only appears with three.

AttackResultWhy
Impersonate B to A and guess the answerStoppedTwo answer words from 256 without replacement: about 1 in 65,000 per attempt, and each attempt burns a line and ends the call.
Replay answers recorded from an earlier checkStoppedA line is never used twice. A recorded line is already crossed out on both copies.
Impersonate A to B: "read me line 4"StoppedThe answerer requires the ask word first. Guessing it is a 1 in 256 chance, and a wrong guess is itself an alarm.
Harvest: impersonate B to A to learn an ask word, then use it on B to learn the answer, then call A againStoppedA always moves to a fresh line. The harvested answer belongs to a line A has already crossed out.
Cross-pair leak: learn an ask word from A, use it on B to get an answer, use that answer on CStoppedOnly possible if tables are shared by more than two people. §2 rule 1 forbids that. (The first draft of this protocol used one family-wide table and failed this attack.)
Predict which line will be askedStoppedThe line order is public and doesn't matter: every line's words are independent and random.
Live relay: call A as B and B as A at the same time and pass words between themNot stoppedPaper can't detect a relay. Mitigation: after a pass, for anything large, call back on a known contact anyway.
Card lost, stolen or photographedNot stoppedWhoever holds a copy can pass. Replace every table the card covered. A photo on a cloud-synced phone counts as a copy.
Coercion: someone with the real person forces them to answerNot stoppedOutside the model. Families MAY agree a separate duress word.

5Uses

The same table and exchange, with different pairs:

  • Family emergency calls. The cloned-voice "grandchild in trouble" call. The person most often targeted needs only paper.
  • Payment-detail changes between businesses. A supplier's accounts contact and a customer's accounts contact hold a table. "We've changed our bank details" requires a check before any payment moves.
  • Help desks. An employee and their manager or IT contact hold a table, so a password reset or MFA re-enrolment requested by phone can be checked.
  • Newsrooms and sources. A reporter and a source meet once, exchange a table, and can confirm each other later on any channel without either holding a key on a device.

6Word list v1

256 words, so each word is exactly one random byte. Selection rules: concrete nouns a child would know; at most 9 letters; no homophones; no rhyming pairs that are easy to mishear on a phone line (removed: bagel/bugle, rocket/pocket/locket, carrot/parrot, lizard/wizard and others). Groups that share a four-letter ending were reviewed by ear one at a time. The list's order is the canonical index for implementations.

7Prior art

None of the parts are new. Military radio operators have authenticated voice traffic with printed challenge-and-reply tables (DRYAD, KTC 1400) for decades. Banks posted numbered one-time code lists (TAN and iTAN). S/KEY and OTPW printed one-time password lists for computer logins. Families are told to agree a safe word, and printable safe-word cards exist. Several apps (TrustWord, Trust Onion, Family Code Word and others) rotate codes between paired phones.

What this protocol adds is the combination: a table on paper only, one table per pair of people, one use per line, and an ask word so that the person asking has to show they hold a copy too, with the rules written so both sides can follow them without a device. We found no earlier description of this combination after a dozen searches, but that is not proof that none exists.
